I've been noticing a pattern lately: as technology becomes more capable, human attention becomes more valuable.

That may sound counterintuitive at first. We tend to think of new technology as a way of reducing work, but in practice what often happens is that technology increases what a business is capable of doing. More customers can be reached, more channels can be managed, more opportunities become available. The possibilities expand, yet human attention does not. Whether a business has one employee or twenty, there is still a finite amount of attention available to make decisions, coordinate work, solve problems, and serve customers. And that reality feels increasingly important.

Spend enough time around small business owners and you'll notice something interesting. Their days are rarely consumed by one big problem; instead, they're filled with dozens of small ones. A customer wants an update on an order. Someone else has a question about pricing. A supplier needs confirmation, a payment needs to be verified, and an enquiry arrives through Instagram while another arrives through WhatsApp.

Nothing feels particularly difficult, and most of it is familiar work. The challenge comes from the accumulation. A business owner starts the day intending to focus on growth and ends it having spent most of their time simply keeping things moving. There is a difference between the two: growth requires attention, but so does maintenance, and only one of them expands the business.

## Every business is a set of systems

This is one reason I've become increasingly interested in systems. The word itself sounds cold and mechanical, but every successful business eventually becomes a collection of them. The process used to fulfil orders is a system. The way new customers are acquired is a system. So is the way support is provided, and the way knowledge is shared within a team. The businesses that continue growing are often the ones that become intentional about these systems, because systems create leverage.

## Effort keeps you up, leverage moves you forward

Leverage is one of those words that gets used often and understood rarely. At its core, though, it is simple: leverage allows effort invested once to create value many times over. A well-trained employee, a documented process, a delivery network, or a software tool each creates leverage. The form changes, but the principle remains the same. The most successful businesses throughout history have been remarkable accumulators of leverage, finding ways to extend their capabilities beyond the limits of individual effort.

This is where the current wave of technology becomes particularly interesting. For decades, large organisations enjoyed advantages that smaller businesses could only dream of, with specialised teams, sophisticated tools, and operational systems built to handle complexity at scale. Smaller businesses often relied on something else: people working incredibly hard. The business kept moving because people kept pushing.

There is something admirable about that, and hard work will always matter. Yet there is a difference between effort and leverage. Effort allows a business to keep up; leverage allows a business to move forward.

## When leverage becomes affordable

What excites me about this moment is that leverage is becoming dramatically more accessible. Capabilities that once required entire departments can increasingly be reached by small teams, and processes that previously depended on constant human intervention can now be designed to operate more consistently. Businesses can build systems that help them stay responsive as they grow, rather than becoming overwhelmed by their own success.

The implications extend far beyond efficiency. When a team spends less time handling repetitive work, it creates more room for work that requires judgment, creativity, and human connection. When information flows more easily through a business, decisions happen faster. And when customers receive timely responses, opportunities are less likely to be missed. In other words, the benefits compound.

That word, **compound**, is important. Much of business growth comes from compounding advantages: a process improvement made today continues creating value months from now, and a system implemented once continues supporting the business long after the initial effort has been forgotten. This is why leverage matters so much. Its benefits accumulate.

## A more level playing field

I've come to believe that one of the most significant shifts happening in business today has very little to do with AI itself. The more interesting story is who gets access to leverage. For the first time, many small and medium-sized businesses can reach capabilities that were historically reserved for much larger organisations, and the gap between what a large company can do and what a smaller business can do is beginning to narrow.

That is a meaningful change, because small businesses create jobs, support families, and keep local economies moving. They sit at the heart of the communities they serve. So when they gain access to better tools, stronger systems, and new forms of leverage, the effects rarely stop at the business itself. The benefits ripple outward.

So much of the work that fills a business owner's day is necessary but repetitive: the countless small interactions that keep things running while quietly competing for attention. The opportunity in front of us is not to replace the people doing that work, but to give them leverage, so their judgment and creativity can reach further than effort alone ever could.

Because the future won't belong to the businesses that work the hardest. It will belong to the businesses that learn how to combine human ingenuity with systems. And for the first time, that leverage is becoming accessible to businesses of every size.
