Lately, I've been thinking about what makes a community thrive.

It's one of those questions that seems straightforward until you spend enough time with it.

Most of us can recognize a thriving community when we see one. There are opportunities. Businesses are opening rather than closing. People feel optimistic about the future. Young people can imagine a life for themselves without having to look elsewhere for it.

The usual explanations make sense. Education matters. Infrastructure matters. Governance matters.

Yet the more I think about it, the more I find myself drawn to a different question: what actually turns the potential within a community into prosperity?

A community can be full of talented, ambitious, hardworking people and still struggle economically. Potential, by itself, doesn't change much. It has to be translated into something useful.

That's where businesses come in.

Every business begins with someone identifying a problem and deciding to solve it. A baker feeds people. A tailor clothes them. A merchant connects them to products they need. The specifics vary, but the underlying dynamic is the same. Someone creates value for someone else.

Scale that across thousands of businesses and something interesting happens. Communities become networks of value creation. People earn livelihoods by helping other people meet their needs.

The strongest communities I've seen tend to have this quality. There is a constant exchange taking place. Skills become services. Ideas become products. Effort becomes income. People are participating in the economic life around them rather than watching it happen from the sidelines.

Small businesses sit at the center of that activity.

What makes them particularly interesting is that their impact rarely ends with the business owner. A growing business creates opportunities for employees, suppliers, contractors, and other businesses around it. The benefits spread outward, often in ways that are difficult to measure but easy to feel.

This feels especially relevant in places like Nigeria and many other developing economies.

We often talk about a shortage of jobs, capital, or infrastructure. What strikes me is that there is no shortage of talent. Ambition is everywhere. Creativity is everywhere. The challenge is creating enough pathways for those qualities to become productive economic activity.

Small businesses are one of those pathways.

For many people, they provide a first job, a first customer, a first apprenticeship, or a first glimpse into entrepreneurship itself. They create entry points into the economy that would not otherwise exist.

That is why support for small businesses matters.

It is an investment in a community's productive capacity.

When entrepreneurs have access to better tools, better systems, and better opportunities, their ability to create value expands. The effects are rarely confined to the business itself. They show up in households, neighbourhoods, and local economies.

A thriving business can employ dozens of people over its lifetime. A thriving ecosystem of businesses can transform an entire community.

Of course, businesses alone cannot solve every societal challenge. Communities also need strong institutions, quality education, reliable infrastructure, and effective leadership. Economic activity does not replace those things.

It does, however, provide the foundation upon which many of them depend.

When people can earn, build, and create value for one another, communities become more resilient. They become more optimistic. They become more capable of shaping their own future.

Perhaps that is why thriving businesses and thriving communities are so closely connected.

At their best, both are expressions of the same idea: people contributing to one another's progress.

And when enough people have the opportunity to do that, communities don't simply grow.

They flourish.
